# EDA Findings — india_housing_prices.csv

Generated by `python -m scripts.run_eda`. Interactive charts: `reports/eda_report.html`.

## Key takeaways

- The data is clean but synthetic-looking: no missing values or duplicates, but every column behaves like an independent random draw.
- Price is unrelated to size, location, BHK, amenities, transport, or any other field (all |r| < 0.01). Learned valuation models cannot beat predicting the average (R² ≈ 0).
- ₹/sqft varies only because price and size vary independently, so large homes look cheap per sqft by arithmetic.
- Many records are internally inconsistent (floor above building height in 46% of rows; 'under construction' homes built years ago). They are kept and flagged.
- Implication: PropWise compares a listing to its comparable cohort using a transparent rule and projects value with explicit growth scenarios. It does not claim to predict real returns.

## 1. Distribution of property prices

**What it shows.** Number of listings in each ₹ lakh price band.

**Main finding.** Prices run from ₹10.0 L to ₹500.0 L with a median of ₹253.87 L. The histogram is flat (skew 0.008), with every band holding about the same number of listings. That is what a uniform random draw looks like, not a real market's typical right-skewed shape.

**Why it matters.** Investors usually expect many mid-market homes and a thin luxury tail. Here no price band is more 'typical' than another, so 'cheap' or 'expensive' only makes sense relative to comparable properties.

**Limitations.** One snapshot with no dates. Prices are asking prices, not transaction prices.

## 2. Distribution of property sizes

**What it shows.** Number of listings in each size band (sqft).

**Main finding.** Sizes run from 500 to 5,000 sqft (median 2,747). The distribution is again flat (skew 0.001).

**Why it matters.** Size is the denominator of ₹/sqft, so it shapes every value comparison in this product.

**Limitations.** Size is not linked to BHK: 44.5% of 1-BHK listings exceed 3,000 sqft, which is implausible.

## 3. Price per sqft by property type

**What it shows.** Spread of ₹/sqft (quartiles, 5th–95th percentile whiskers) for apartments, independent houses and villas.

**Main finding.** Median ₹/sqft: Apartment ₹9,245, Independent House ₹9,305, Villa ₹9,190. The three distributions are practically identical.

**Why it matters.** In real markets villas and apartments carry different per-sqft prices. Here property type does not separate value, so comparisons still stay within type to be safe.

**Limitations.** The dataset behaves like independently generated columns, so the absence of a relationship here says nothing about real Indian housing markets.

## 4. Relationship between size and price

**What it shows.** A 4,000-listing sample of size against asking price, with the binned mean price.

**Main finding.** Correlation is r = -0.0025, so larger homes are not more expensive. As a direct consequence, size and ₹/sqft are strongly negatively correlated (r = -0.615).

**Why it matters.** This is the single most important fact for interpreting results. Large homes look 'cheap per sqft' purely arithmetically. The good-value label compares like-for-like cohorts but cannot remove the effect inside a cohort.

**Limitations.** Scatter is sampled for speed. Correlation captures linear association only.

## 5. Outliers in price per sqft and size

**What it shows.** Distribution of ₹/sqft with the Tukey upper fence (Q3 + 1.5×IQR).

**Main finding.** 19,723 listings (7.9%) sit above ₹32,764/sqft. 74% of them are under 1,000 sqft, so they are small homes, not data-entry errors. Size has no outliers (bounded 500–5,000).

**Why it matters.** Outliers are kept because they come from the same generating process as the rest. Tree models and median-based benchmarks are robust to them.

**Limitations.** The fence is a convention, not a rule for 'wrong' data.

**Why it matters.** Real state-level differences (e.g. Maharashtra vs. Bihar) are large. Their absence confirms that location carries no price signal in this data.

## 8. Median property age by locality

**What it shows.** Distribution of median building age across the 20,999 City–Locality pairs (locality IDs repeat in every city, so the pair is the real unit).

**Main finding.** Half of localities have a median age between 15 and 22 years. Each locality has only about 12 listings, so locality medians are noisy.

**Why it matters.** Locality-level statistics would be over-fitted, so the models use city-level cohorts instead.

**Limitations.** Small samples per locality, and locality IDs are anonymised.

## 9. BHK distribution across cities

**What it shows.** Share of each city's listings by BHK.

**Main finding.** Every cell lies between 18.6% and 22.0%: each city has a near-even 20% split across 1–5 BHK.

**Why it matters.** Unit mix normally differs by city (more 1–2 BHK in metros). An even mix means cohorts are well balanced, at least 165 training listings each.

**Limitations.** The dataset behaves like independently generated columns, so the absence of a relationship here says nothing about real Indian housing markets.

## 10. Most expensive localities

**What it shows.** The 15 City–Locality pairs with the highest median ₹/sqft (pairs with ≥10 listings).

**Main finding.** Top: Durgapur / Locality_164 at ₹33,025/sqft. Locality medians have a standard deviation of ₹2,775. If prices are shuffled randomly across listings, it is ₹2,756, so locality differences are what random assignment alone produces.

**Why it matters.** 'Top localities' here are mostly small-sample luck and would likely regress to the mean. Do not read them as premium neighbourhoods.

**Limitations.** The brief asked for price trends of top localities, but there is no time dimension, so trends cannot be computed.

## 11. Correlation among numeric variables

**What it shows.** Pearson correlation for every pair of numeric fields.

**Main finding.** Only the two mechanical relationships stand out: Price_per_SqFt_INR ~ Size_in_SqFt r = -0.61; Price_in_Lakhs ~ Price_per_SqFt_INR r = +0.56. Every other pair has |r| < 0.01 (Year Built and Age, r = −1 by definition, is excluded).

**Why it matters.** No attribute moves with price, which is why the regression signal test finds R² ≈ 0.

**Limitations.** Correlation is linear and pairwise. The models test non-linear and interaction effects separately (still R² ≈ 0).
